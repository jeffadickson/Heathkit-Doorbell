# The Heathkit TD-1089 Programmable Electronic Chimes

*For Mom, who always wanted bells.*

I built this kit for her when I was about ten, on the kitchen floor. My uncle had to resolder all my cold joints, and I never made another one. It sounded terrible. She kept it up for years anyway, and now I understand why.

## The kit

Heath sold the TD-1089 from the mid-1970s into at least 1989. The schematic is dated 1975, and the doorbell was in the catalog by fall 1977. In the Fall 1978 catalog it was $44.95, about $231 in August 2026 dollars; in 1984 it was $49.95, about $161. Heath's pitch: a one-octave C-to-C "keyboard" that plays any tune of up to 16 notes, wired between your existing bell transformer and the unit.

Catalog specifications: 16 VAC at 10 VA from the bell transformer; a 13-note by 16-beat programming matrix, with the same note on at most 9 beats; a "decaying, mellow" tone; a 3 × 5 inch, 16 Ω oval speaker; 6 W idle and 12 W maximum; a brown plastic cabinet with gold trim and tan fabric panels.

## Programming it

Sixteen flying leads, A1 to A16, one per beat. Thirteen rows of sockets, one row per note, nine sockets to a row. You pushed each lead into a socket in the row for the note you wanted on that beat, and left it out for a rest. Nine sockets per row is where the "same note up to 9 beats" limit comes from.

It was tedious. There was no feedback until you pressed the button; a wrong note meant tracing sixteen similar wires to find the one in the wrong row, and every new tune was a rewire.

## The circuit

All from schematic 595-1826-05. The TTL part numbers are inferred from Heath stock numbers and pinouts.

**Button latches (IC1, 7404).** Each door button pulls a terminal that is fed from +20 V through 680 Ω. Two inverters with a 270 Ω feedback resistor form a latch, so a tap keeps the tune running. The counter is a 7493, whose two reset inputs must both be asserted to hold it at zero; Heath gave one to each door's latch, so either button frees it. A 0.01 µF capacitor (C1, C2) sits on each button input.

**Beat generator (Q1, Q2).** A PNP/NPN pair wired as a regenerative switch, the discrete equivalent of a unijunction oscillator. C3 (0.68 µF) charges through R11 (150 kΩ) plus the 2 MΩ SPEED pot R9; from the part values that is about 0.15 to 2 seconds per beat. Q3 buffers the pulse.

**Counter and decoder (IC2 7493, IC3 74154).** The 7493 counts 0 to 15; the 74154 turns the count into sixteen active-low lines. Each line reaches its lead through its own diode (D3 to D18). Only the selected line is low; the other fifteen sit high with their diodes reverse-biased.

**Two doors.** Decoder output 15 resets the front latch, so the front door plays all sixteen beats. The back door's reset is a jumper to one of IC3 pins 4 to 9, so the back door plays only the opening four to nine beats of the same tune.

**The note ladder (R31 to R44).** The plugged lead grounds one tap of a series string: 27 k, then 3300, 3600, 3600, 3900, 3900, 4300, 4700, 4700, 5100, 5600, 5600 and 6200 Ω. The oscillator's timing resistance is the 50 kΩ TUNING pot (R45) plus the string down to that tap, so the pitch goes as 1 / (R_offset + R_string). With an offset of about 27.25 kΩ, these stock 5 % values land every note within ±4 cents of equal temperament. There are no per-note trimmers; the string itself is the scale.

**Tone oscillator (Q7, Q8, Q6).** The same complementary-pair oscillator as the beat generator, with a 0.01 µF timing capacitor (C8). Q6 squares its pulses into the TONE line. An unplugged lead leaves the ladder floating, the oscillator stalls, and the beat is a rest.

**Octave divider (IC4, 7493).** A second 7493 divides the tone by 2, 4, 8 and 16. The ÷4 output is the note you hear, summed in through 15 kΩ; ÷8 through 330 kΩ and ÷16 through 1 MΩ add a faint sub-octave. The beat pulse resets this counter, so every note starts in phase.

**Decay (Q4, Q5, C4).** On each beat Q4 dumps C4. C4 then recharges from the ÷2 output through R21 (27 kΩ), the 500 kΩ DECAY pot R22 and D19, turning Q5 on gradually. Q5 shunts the summed tone to ground, so the note fades as it conducts: a shunt transistor used as a VCA.

**Filtering and amplifier.** The square waves pass through two RC poles, about 1.1 kHz at the summing node and about 340 Hz at R27 (47 kΩ) with C6 (0.01 µF). That strips most of the harmonics, which is where "mellow" came from, and why it sounded soft and dull through a 3 × 5 speaker. IC5 (Heath 442-610) on the +14 V rail drives the speaker through 50 µF.

**Power (Q9, Q10).** D22 half-wave rectifies the 16 VAC into 1200 µF for +20 V. Zener-referenced emitter followers make +5 V (6.2 V zener) and +14 V (15 V zener), each with a 5 W resistor in parallel to share the current. Always-on TTL accounts for the 6 W idle draw.

## The designer

Terry Perdue, K8TP, was a Heath design engineer from 1973 to 1991, in the amateur radio group; his best-known ham product is the HD-1780 Intellirotor. He built the doorbell prototype at home after a colleague, Joe Huber, introduced him to digital logic. Heath wanted it, but Director of Engineering Ray Freridge would not let a ham-group engineer kit a non-ham product, so Bjorn Heyning in the General products group finished it. Heyning cut cost by replacing Perdue's posts and snap-on clips with rows of cheap wire sockets and, as Perdue recalled, used button inputs with little hysteresis; the schematic shows plain 7404 inverters there, not Schmitt triggers.

Perdue's proofbuild rang when the button was pressed. It also rang, or played back his voice, when he transmitted on certain ham bands, and it rang every time the toilet flushed: the house was on a well with a small pressure tank, so each flush started the pump and the line glitch coupled into the long doorbell wiring. A bypass capacitor on each button input cured both problems. The production schematic shows exactly that, C1 and C2. He insisted the "TD" did not stand for Toilet Detector.

Perdue later became Heath's historian. He self-published *Heath Nostalgia* in 1992 and later the CD *Heathkit: The Early Years*.

## The emulator

`web/index.html` models the circuit as drawn:

- the ladder, with optional ±5 % part tolerances;
- ÷4, ÷8 and ÷16 square waves weighted 15 k : 330 k : 1 M;
- an exponential shunt decay, restarted on every beat and cut when the counter moves on;
- one-pole filters at 1.1 kHz and 340 Hz;
- an optional 3 × 5 speaker model with 60 Hz ripple from the half-wave supply.

Its wire board reproduces the socket layout: sixteen leads off the beat bar, thirteen rows of nine holes.
