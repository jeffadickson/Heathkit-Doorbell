Programmable Electronic Chimes

The Heathkit TD-1089 replaced the ding-dong with any tune you could fit into sixteen beats. You programmed it by plugging a wire lead from each beat into a one-octave row of note sockets. No microprocessor, no ROM: three TTL chips, a dozen transistors, and a string of thirteen resistors.

Below is a working model built from Heath’s schematic. Rewire the matrix, press the front or back door button, and hear it the way the circuit made it: octave-divided square waves, a shunt-transistor decay, a 340 Hz RC filter, and a 3×5-inch, 16-ohm oval speaker.

The emulator

Program it, then ring it

Each column is one beat lead, A1 to A16. Click a socket to plug that lead into a note; click it again to unplug it, which makes a rest. The right-hand count shows sockets used per note. The knobs carry their schematic names.

Drag a plug (the end of a wire, or the terminal at the top) into any free hole in a note’s row. Drop it off the board to leave that beat silent. Wire colours follow the resistor code by beat.

How it worked · from schematic 595-1826-05

Three TTL chips and a resistor string

Button latches IC1 · 7404

Each doorbell button pulls a terminal fed from +20 V through 680 Ω. Two inverters of a 7404 with a 270 Ω feedback resistor form a latch, so a tap of the button keeps the tune running. The counter is a 7493, which has two reset inputs that must both be active to hold it at zero. Heath gave one to each door’s latch, so either button frees the counter.

Beat generator Q1 · Q2

A PNP/NPN pair wired as a regenerative switch, the discrete version of a unijunction oscillator. C3 (0.68 µF) charges through R11 (150K) plus the 2 MΩ SPEED pot until Q1 fires and dumps it. From the part values that is roughly 0.15 to 2 seconds per beat. Q3 buffers the pulse.

Counter and decoder IC2 · IC3

The 7493 counts beats 0–15 and a 74154 turns the count into sixteen active-low lines. Each line goes through its own diode (D3–D18) to a flying lead. Only the selected line is low; the other fifteen sit high with their diodes reverse-biased, so leads plugged elsewhere stay out of the way.

The two doors reset wiring

Decoder output 15 resets the front latch, so the front door plays the whole sixteen. The back door latch is reset by a wire you attach to one of IC3 pins 4–9, so it plays only the opening four to nine beats of the same tune. One program, two lengths.

The note ladder R31–R44

The plugged lead grounds one tap on a series string of thirteen resistors. The tone oscillator’s timing resistance is everything from the 50K TUNING pot down the string to that tap, so lower rows mean more resistance and a lower note. No per-note trimmers: the string itself is the scale. See the table below for how good it is.

Tone oscillator Q7 · Q8 · Q6

The same complementary-pair trick as the beat generator, with a 0.01 µF timing cap (C8), producing a train of narrow pulses. Q6 squares them up into the TONE line. An unplugged lead leaves the ladder floating, the oscillator stalls, and you get a rest for free.

Octave divider IC4 · 7493

A second 7493 divides the tone by 2, 4, 8 and 16. The ÷4 output is the note you hear, fed in through 15K. The ÷8 and ÷16 outputs are mixed in quietly through 330K and 1M, a faint organ-style sub-octave. The beat pulse resets this counter too, so every note starts in phase.

Decay Q4 · Q5 · C4

On each beat, Q4 dumps C4. C4 then recharges from the ÷2 square wave through R21 (27K), the 500K DECAY pot and D19, and its voltage turns Q5 on gradually. Q5 shunts the summed tone to ground, so the note fades as Q5 conducts. A shunt transistor as a VCA: cheap, and it gives the struck-chime fade.

Why it sounded “mellow” R27 · C5 · C6

The square waves go through two RC poles: about 1.1 kHz at the summing node, then R27 (47K) with C6 (0.01 µF), a corner near 340 Hz. That removes most of the harmonics, which is why it sounded soft and a little dull, especially through a 3×5 speaker. IC5 (Heath 442-610) on the +14 V rail drives the speaker through 50 µF.

Power Q9 · Q10

One diode (D22) half-wave rectifies the 16 VAC bell transformer into 1200 µF for +20 V. Two zener-referenced emitter followers make +5 V (6.2 V zener) and +14 V (15 V zener), each with a 5 W resistor in parallel to carry part of the current. TTL burning power around the clock is where the 6 W idle draw went.

The ladder is a nice piece of design. Pitch goes as 1 / (Rtune + Rstring). With the TUNING pot and oscillator contributing about 27.25K, these off-the-shelf 5% values land every note within about ±4 cents of equal temperament, finer than most ears can hear. Ascending the scale, each step needs about 6% more resistance than the last, so the values climb from 3300 to 6200 Ω. Error column: nominal values with the offset fitted for best match. Real 5% parts add more; the emulator’s “real ladder” mode rolls its own tolerances.

Part identities: Heath stock numbers 443-640 and 443-623 match the 7493 and 74154 by pinout (Vcc on pin 5 and pins 20–24 respectively), and IC1’s pin pairs match a 7404. IC5 is listed only as Heath 442-610; I haven’t pinned down the industry number.

The designer

Terry Perdue, K8TP

Perdue was a Heath design engineer from 1973 to 1991, in the amateur radio group. His best-known ham product is the HD-1780 Intellirotor, a computerized antenna-rotator controller. He built the doorbell prototype at home after a Heath colleague, Joe Huber, got him into digital logic.

Heath wanted the doorbell, but Director of Engineering Ray Freridge wouldn’t let a ham-group engineer kit a non-ham product. It went to Bjorn Heyning in the General products group, who cut cost by replacing Perdue’s posts and snap-on clips with rows of cheap wire sockets and, as Perdue remembered it, used simpler button inputs with little hysteresis. The schematic agrees: plain 7404 inverters on the button lines, not Schmitt triggers.

Perdue’s own proofbuild rang when the button was pressed, rang (or played back his voice) when he transmitted on certain ham bands, and rang every time the toilet flushed. The house ran on a well with a small pressure tank, so each flush started the pump, and the glitch coupled into the long doorbell wiring. A bypass capacitor on each button input cured it. The production schematic has exactly that: 0.01 µF, C1 and C2, on both door inputs.

He closed his story by insisting the “TD” didn’t really stand for Toilet Detector.
He became Heath’s historian. After leaving Heath, Perdue self-published Heath Nostalgia (1992), a 124-page company history with factory photos and recollections from long-time employees, and later a CD, Heathkit: The Early Years, with roughly a thousand scanned photos, catalogs and documents plus a 30-minute recording of Heath’s first Director of Engineering, Gene Fiebich. Both are out of print. Former Heath VP Chas Gilmore has called Perdue’s the most accurate of the Heath histories.

For an EMC engineer it is a tidy case: high-impedance inputs, long unshielded runs, and no hysteresis, with two failure paths (conducted transient and RF pickup) and one capacitor that fixed both.
